25,000 Rocket Kits a Year, With No
Contract Signed Yet

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 20, 2026 3 min read

On October 19, the Department of War announced a framework agreement with BAE Systems to ramp up production of the Advanced Precision Kill Weapon System, known as APKWS. The headline number is 25,000 guidance sections a year for up to seven years. Read the announcement closely and a second sentence does the real work: the procurement is pending congressional authorization and appropriations. No multiyear contract has been signed. Neither side disclosed a price.

What the Kit Does

An APKWS guidance section fits between the warhead and the motor of an unguided 2.75 inch rocket and turns it into a laser guided munition. All four US services field it, according to the department, and it has become the cheaper tool for shooting down drones, a job that otherwise burns air to air missiles costing far more per shot. BAE builds the kits in Hudson, New Hampshire, and Austin, Texas, and said in February 2026 that it had delivered its 100,000th kit. Michael P. Duffey, the Under Secretary of War for Acquisition and Sustainment, sold the framework in cost terms: "By securing multi-year demand, this agreement keeps production lines running at capacity, provides our forces with interceptor inventory, and flips the cost calculus in the taxpayer's favor."

The One Number to Keep in Proportion

That number is 25,000, and it is not new capacity. Defense News noted on October 19 that the figure matches capacity BAE has cited before: a company official said in 2023 that APKWS was in full rate production with room to grow to 25,000 units a year. The framework also outlines a path to 35,000 kits a year for allies, and that extra output only arrives as partner nations file formal requests through the Foreign Military Sales program. So the announcement formalizes a ceiling BAE already claimed, extends it across seven years, and leaves the funding to Congress.

What Is Actually Funded

The money on the books is older and smaller. The Navy's current five year APKWS contract with BAE, awarded in August 2025, is worth up to $1.74 billion and covers up to 55,000 kits for the Navy, the Army and foreign customers, with work expected to finish by December 2031. Allied demand is real but still mostly paperwork. Saudi Arabia won State Department approval in July 2026 for a possible $1.96 billion package that includes up to 20,000 kits, split evenly between air to air and air to ground versions. Italy is weighing a buy of 5,031 kits for counter drone defense. BAE Systems (BA.) books revenue on signed contracts. Approvals and intentions do not enter backlog.

My position: own the distinction. The framework is a demand signal from the Pentagon's acquisition shop, and demand signals have been reliable predictors in this munitions cycle. The January framework for THAAD interceptors became a contract worth more than $35 billion in June. But until Congress authorizes and funds a multiyear APKWS contract, treat the 25,000 a year figure as a capacity number. Watch for the multiyear contract itself, and for Saudi Arabia converting its July approval into a signed case. Either event turns this announcement into backlog. Neither has happened yet.
