18 Tanker Attacks and Brent
Still Only at $110

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 21, 2026 4 min read

On October 20, Iran's Revolutionary Guards announced that their naval mines had struck three tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and set two of them ablaze. Reuters reported the next morning that Brent crude was trading at $110.38 a barrel, up 1.5 percent. I keep reading that the market is carrying a risk premium that any credible ceasefire talk could remove quickly. The numbers I am looking at say the opposite. At $110, Brent is trading below what the physical damage already costs to move a barrel.

The consensus view

The consensus version goes like this. Prices dipped on October 20 after Iran's president sat down with Pakistan's army chief in Tehran to discuss a Qatari backed de-escalation plan, and traders are holding a geopolitical fear bid that will unwind the moment talks turn serious. Suvro Sarkar, head of energy research at DBS, described the current action as "a mix of profit taking and optimistic hopes of a Qatar or Pakistan brokered deal, countered by the realisation that any deal is still very distant." The polite reading of that sentence is that the market is waiting for a headline, and the headline would send Brent back toward $100.

Why that reading is wrong

Start with what is already physical, not hypothetical. Ship-tracker Kpler reported on Monday that at least 18 vessel attacks have been recorded over the past two weeks. LSEG analysts warned the same day that Iran is now targeting tankers well beyond the strait, into the Gulf of Oman, threatening the crude loadings outside the Persian Gulf and the ship-to-ship transfers that had become the industry's workaround for moving oil around the blocked route. Read that again: the bypass itself is being attacked. The strait is only the start of the problem now. The attacks have spread to the shipping lanes beyond it, and shipping lanes do not reopen because a delegation lands in Tehran.

Then there is the freight math, which does not care about your optimism. A Goldman Sachs report published Tuesday puts the global average crude freight rate above $20 a barrel, against roughly $3 a year earlier. About $7 of that increase is the war risk premium for crossing the strait. Another $4 comes from the ship-to-ship transfers outside the Gulf. That $17 of transport cost sits on every barrel that moves, whether talks happen tomorrow or never. Goldman also warned that elevated shipping costs are widening price differentials between crude grades and squeezing refiners' margins, with Asian refineries taking the worst of it. A premium you can remove with a press release is one thing. A per-barrel logistics tax baked into every voyage is another.

The cushion is gone

The third piece is inventory. Market sources citing American Petroleum Institute data said US crude stocks fell 4.9 million barrels in the week ended October 16. On Tuesday, Reuters reported that two Energy Department officials were working with refiners around the world to boost fuel output because a diesel shortage has pushed prices higher. A market sitting on full storage can absorb a few burning tankers and wait for diplomacy. A market drawing down stocks while officials scramble for extra refinery output cannot.

The IRGC's own statement on Tuesday shows the intent to keep it that way. Its Sepah News channel quoted the force directly: "Two violating oil tankers were set ablaze and halted due to the explosion of mines planted by the brave sailors of the IRGC Navy at an illegal crossing point in the southern part of the Strait of Hormuz." That is a navy advertising its blockade, not a government preparing to dismantle it. Iran told Pakistan's army chief the same day that there would be no talks with Washington until Tehran's demands are met. The Houthis added their own Monday claims, taking credit for strikes on a Saudi Aramco site in Yanbu, the Shaybah oilfield, and airports in Riyadh and Abha.

My position: sell the relief rally. The trade that prices in "a deal removes the premium" treats October's events as one shock event with a discrete end. What Kpler, LSEG and Goldman describe is a new cost structure: double digit freight per barrel, a shrinking geography of safe water, and falling inventories. The winners in that cost structure are not the oil majors, who eat refinery margin. They are the tanker owners, who collect the freight. That is why this issue is filed under Frontline (FRO) rather than an upstream name: when the strait becomes a toll booth, the toll collector is the equity to watch.
