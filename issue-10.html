China Just Locked the
Solar Factory Door

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 18, 2026 3 min read

On Friday, China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology published a draft revision of the national export control catalogue, covering 40 technologies. The Associated Press count breaks down as 23 artificial intelligence technologies, 12 solar technologies and 5 biomedical technologies. Most of the coverage led with the AI rules. I read the solar pages twice, because those are the ones attached to factories, financing and listed earnings.

What happened

Under the draft, reported by Reuters on October 18, China would ban exports of technology for thin wafers, perovskite and tandem solar cells, and large-scale fluidized-bed polysilicon production. Fluidized-bed is the cheaper, less energy hungry route to polysilicon, the base material for most panels. Advanced cell manufacturing technology, including TOPCon, HJT and XBC, would be restricted rather than banned. The ministry said solar technology needs greater oversight because it is increasingly used in aerospace and national defense, and said the wider package aims to "guard against the risks of technologies from running out of control or being improperly disseminated." Exporters must register certain overseas technology transfers until the catalogue is formally updated. Public consultation closes on November 15.

The AI half of the draft bans exports of core technology for models trained on sensitive national data, and it would restrict models of at least two trillion parameters or with world-leading capability. The draft names no models. The Associated Press notes that Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 and Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max exceed the size threshold. Omdia analyst Lian Jye Su told the AP the move could respond to Western sovereignty requirements for key technologies. Natixis economist Gary Ng put it more plainly: access to technology is fragmenting along with trade and investment.

Why the solar clause carries the money

Reuters reported in April that Chinese officials had already held initial talks with manufacturers about curbs on advanced solar-panel manufacturing technology, a move that could threaten plans by US firms such as Tesla (TSLA) to expand production in the United States. Beijing has also curbed exports of solar-manufacturing technology to India, according to Reuters. So this draft formalizes a squeeze that was already being applied informally, and it lands while the US and India are spending heavily to build crystalline silicon capacity that still leans on Chinese process know-how.

The AI clause matters less on Monday than the headlines suggest. Moonshot is private. Alibaba (BABA) is the listed name anywhere near the threshold, and a draft that names no models gives Beijing discretion rather than an immediate earnings hit. The solar clause is different: it raises the cost and the timeline for every rival factory that planned to buy its way to competence, and it protects the margin of the incumbent who never needed the purchase.

My position: own the manufacturer outside the crystalline silicon chain. First Solar (FSLR) makes cadmium telluride thin-film modules. Its product uses no polysilicon and no crystalline silicon, and its factories sit in the United States. Every month Beijing spends making Chinese solar know-how harder to export is a month in which the gap between First Solar's existing plants and a rival's planned plant gets harder to close with money alone.

The one number

40. That is the count of technologies in one draft catalogue, and the spread tells you the intent. Beijing is applying the export control method Washington built for chips to the industries where China holds the incumbent position: frontier models, solar manufacturing and cell and gene therapy. Controls used to flow one way. As of Friday, they are a shared vocabulary.

What to watch

Watch November 15, when public consultation closes, and then the final catalogue wording on perovskite and fluidized-bed polysilicon. A ban that survives consultation intact delays rival capacity; a ban softened into licensing is negotiating material. Watch also for any comment from Tesla or from Indian manufacturers on equipment and process sourcing, and for whether the registration requirement slows transfers already in progress. The honest answer is that nobody knows how hard the final rule bites until the catalogue is published. The draft alone tells you the direction of travel.
