Brent Lost $8.98 on an Election, Not a Deal

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 5, 2026 4 min read

On Wednesday, November 4, Brent crude settled at $94.62 a barrel, down $8.98, or 8.7 percent, its lowest settlement since August 31. WTI settled at $88.12, down 8.3 percent. Reuters tied the move to a single wager: that the Democratic sweep in Tuesday's midterm elections could bring a quicker end to the eight-month war with Iran. The rest of Wall Street took the same side of the trade. Stocks rallied, and energy shares fell about 4.8 percent, the worst group in the market.

The winners explained it themselves

Vice President JD Vance posted on X on Wednesday: "The reason we lost is the war." He added that anyone calling the conflict worth the cost should ask whether it was worth "losing the Senate for the next 6 years." President Donald Trump told The Wall Street Journal on Tuesday night that "Iran was a piece of it," and said he would have done it again. On Fox News he pointed at the pump instead: voters backed his Iran policy, but they "really probably weren't willing to do the short-term consequence of paying more for gasoline." U.S. retail gasoline remains above $4 a gallon on average nationwide. The market heard a governing party conclude that the war has become politically unaffordable, and it priced the ending on the spot.

Tehran's price did not move

One day earlier, a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Iran and the United States remain far from a deal to end the war, and that Tehran will not accept Washington's "excessive demands." Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf said Tehran had given Washington a "strong and rational response," adding: "The ball is in the US' court." Tehran's stated price for reopening the Strait of Hormuz is specific: waive the oil sanctions, end the U.S. naval blockade, release frozen Iranian funds. Washington has granted none of the three. Overnight before the vote, U.S. forces struck Iran's Qeshm Island, targeting surface-to-air missiles used against U.S. assets. American officials called the strikes defensive and limited. Qatar has stepped up mediation, and Reuters reported altered proposals moving between the sides. An opening demand list, an active mediator and live fire in the strait the day before the election: that was the full state of play when the market cut Brent by $8.98.

The calendar problem

The new Congress does not take its seats until January 3. Michelle Brouhard of Kpler made the point on Wednesday: the administration has almost two months to act before a Democratic majority can constrain war funding. Trump told the Financial Times on Tuesday that the United States has no immediate plans to strike Iran: "We're locked and loaded if we need to go, but there may be no reason for it." Put the two facts side by side. The body that might force an ending cannot vote on one until January, and the president who can order the next strike says he is in no hurry either way. Between an election result and a reopened strait there is a chain of decisions, and no link in that chain carries a date earlier than January 3.

The physical market voted the other way

The Energy Information Administration published its weekly report on Wednesday morning, covering the week ended October 30. Crude stocks fell by 865,000 barrels to 409.8 million barrels, against a Reuters poll that expected an 877,000-barrel build. Distillate stocks fell by 3.6 million barrels to 99.3 million barrels, their lowest level since May 2003, and now sit 13 percent below the five-year average. Total commercial petroleum inventories fell by 8.2 million barrels in one week. Refiners ran at 94.5 percent of capacity, up 1.7 percentage points. Oil fell through all of it, because the session was priced on politics. Distillates deserve a second look from anyone who accepted Wednesday's verdict: diesel and heating oil come out of that 99.3 million barrel stock, and fuel prices are the exact pressure Trump named on Fox News. The tightest tank in the U.S. system is the one connected to the voter's wallet.

What I am pricing

My position: Wednesday sold a conclusion ahead of any event that supports it. Brent fell 7.6 percent on Monday, its biggest one-day drop since late July, on signals that a deal was possible. It fell another 8.7 percent on Wednesday on an election result. Across those two sessions Tehran changed none of its terms, Hormuz stayed shut, and U.S. fuel stocks tightened. A premium that leaves for political reasons can return for physical ones in a single session, and August 31 was the last time Brent settled this low. The honest counterweight: a president who has conceded on the record that the war cost his party Congress has a genuine reason to settle, and the Qatari channel is producing revised proposals rather than statements for the cameras.

Watch three things. First, Brent against $100, the level it settled above as recently as Tuesday at $103.60. Second, Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11, where the United States hosts Russian and Ukrainian delegations; a week of American diplomacy on two wars will show whether the Iran file is moving or waiting. Third, the next EIA report on November 11: a second straight distillate draw at a 23-year low stops being a statistic and becomes the story. Until Tehran moves one of its three terms, I am not shorting the strait.
