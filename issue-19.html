Issue #19 Free Article Silicon Sovereignty

A 10 Percent Chip Price Cut Costs Korea
Up to $100 Billion

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 27, 2026 4 min read

The Bank of Korea put a number on the concentration trade this week. In a written response to lawmaker Ahn Do-geol, reported by Reuters on October 27, the central bank modelled a pessimistic scenario in which South Korean semiconductor exports fall by $50 billion to $100 billion compared with earlier forecasts next year if capital spending on artificial intelligence by major US technology firms slows. The scenario assumes a 10 percent decline in Korean semiconductor unit prices. Read that assumption again. No demand collapse is required.

What the Bank actually said

The Bank said the shortfall would shave about 0.4 percentage point off South Korean GDP growth next year, by driving the current account surplus well below its initial projection of $430 billion for 2027. It named the spending that now carries the forecast: combined capital expenditure by Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle, rising from $40 billion last year to an estimated $80 billion this year and a projected $110 billion next year. Five American budgets have become a Korean macro variable. The Bank added that considerable uncertainty remains over AI demand and the memory cycle. That sentence is doing honest work, and I wish more forecasters wrote it.

The concentration is visible in the trade data before any scenario runs. South Korea exported a record $120.9 billion of goods in September 2026, and semiconductors accounted for $60.3 billion of it, nearly half the total, according to government figures reported by Reuters. Chips account for roughly half of Korean exports in the Bank of Korea framing too. Samsung Electronics (KRX: 005930) and SK Hynix (KRX: 000660) sit at the centre of that flow, supplying the high bandwidth memory that AI data centres consume. When half a country exports one product family to a handful of buyers, price is policy, whether Seoul votes on it or not.

Three futures, with my rough odds

First, the build continues, at about 50 percent odds in my book. The five firms hit something close to the $110 billion projection, memory prices hold, and the Bank of Korea scenario stays a stress test that a lawmaker asked for and an analyst filed away. What breaks this future is a guidance cut from any one of the five, because the market now treats their spending plans as a single correlated number.

Second, spending flattens, at about 30 percent odds. Capex stops growing but does not fall. Orders stretch out, lead times normalise, and Korean export growth slows without a cliff. Earnings at Samsung and SK Hynix cool, the won absorbs part of the shock, and Seoul calls it a soft landing. What breaks this future is pricing: flat volumes with a 10 percent price decline still land inside the Bank of Korea range.

Third, prices slip, at about 20 percent odds. A 10 percent decline in unit prices, the exact assumption in the Bank of Korea model, takes $50 billion to $100 billion off exports versus forecast and 0.4 percentage point off GDP growth. No recession in the United States is needed for this one. A pause in orders and a normal memory downcycle do the job. What breaks this future is a supply response: if the Korean makers cut output fast enough to defend price, the export hit shrinks and the pain moves from revenue to volume.

My position: treat the Bank of Korea document as a concentration map, not as a prediction that AI spending is about to slow. Reuters framed it as a warning, and it is one, but the useful part is the arithmetic. A country can survive a demand shock it can see coming. A 10 percent price move arriving through five foreign budgets is harder to hedge, and it is the exposure hiding inside every bullish note on the memory cycle that quotes export records without quoting this scenario next to them.

What to watch

The next 48 hours give us the first check. SK Hynix reports on October 28 and Samsung Electronics holds its earnings call on October 29. Listen for two things: any change in high bandwidth memory pricing language, and any hint that US customers are pushing delivery schedules into 2027. After that, watch the capex guidance in the US earnings season from the five firms the Bank named. If one of them trims, the Bank of Korea scenario stops being hypothetical on the day the guidance prints.
