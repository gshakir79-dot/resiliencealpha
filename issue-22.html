Japan Gets the Assembly Line, RTX Keeps the Bottleneck

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 30, 2026 4 min read

On Wednesday, October 28, on the sidelines of the ASEAN defense ministers meeting in Cebu, Philippines, Japanese Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi announced that the United States and Japan have finalized a plan to build a final assembly and inspection facility in Japan for the AIM-120 AMRAAM, the air-to-air missile built by Raytheon, a unit of RTX (NYSE: RTX). Koizumi called the missile essential for the F-35 and called the agreement groundbreaking: “This is a groundbreaking agreement that will not only ensure the acquisition of equipment needed by both Japan and the United States, but will also strengthen Japan’s defense industry base.” Breaking Defense reported the announcement on October 29. Read the announcement closely and a pattern appears. It contains no address, no partner, no date, and no number of missiles.

The consensus read, and the missing four facts

The coverage treated this as a production breakthrough, one more proof that allied co-production will refill Western missile magazines. Maybe it will, eventually. What Koizumi actually disclosed on Wednesday was narrower. He did not name the Japanese company that will assemble the missile. He gave no location for the facility, no timeline for opening it, and no expected output. The second half of the announcement was thinner still: the two countries agreed to share the technical information needed to raise production of the Standard Missile-3 Block IIA, and Koizumi did not say by how much production would rise. Both agreements sit under a joint effort the Japanese side calls Operation Supercharge.

Then there is the American half of the record. The Pentagon published a readout of the Tuesday meeting between Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Koizumi, and the readout does not mention the AMRAAM facility or the SM-3 agreement at all. It says Hegseth welcomed Japan’s efforts to strengthen its defense industrial capacity and that both leaders committed to close coordination on critical munitions production. RTX deferred comment to the Defense Department. A Pentagon spokesperson did not respond to Breaking Defense by press time. One government announced a factory. The other government’s account of the same meeting announced coordination. That gap is the most honest sentence in this story, and nobody wrote it on purpose.

The numbers that decide the output rate

The money behind the missile is real, and it has conditions attached. In September, Raytheon won a contract worth up to $20.7 billion over five years, with options for two more years. The Pentagon said at the time that the contract will nearly double AMRAAM production, as long as US lawmakers provide the funding. That clause deserves more weight than it gets. A ceiling is an authorization to spend money Congress still has to appropriate. Under a February framework agreement, annual production is supposed to reach at least 1,900 missiles. Air & Space Forces Magazine puts combined Air Force and Navy procurement at or below 400 missiles a year between fiscal 2017 and fiscal 2022. RTX has spent $80 million on test equipment ahead of the award and says it has been adding capacity for years. None of that is in dispute. What decides whether 1,900 happens sits upstream of final assembly: production has been delayed by component shortages and by the time solid rocket motors take to build, and the new US rocket motor lines RTX points to are expected to come online in 2027.

My position

Final assembly is where a finished missile gets put together and checked. It is the last step in the line, and a second final step in Japan adds resilience against a single site failing. It does not manufacture rocket motors, seekers, or appropriations. Until the motor lines open in 2027 and Congress funds the contract toward its ceiling, output is set by parts and money, and both of those still run through the United States. So I am treating the Japan facility as an option on future capacity, priced by the market this week as capacity itself. Those are different things, and the difference is where the trade lives. Watch for three disclosures, in this order: a named Japanese partner with a site and an opening date, an appropriations figure attached to the $20.7 billion ceiling, and a dated production rate for the SM-3 Block IIA. The first one that arrives with a number in it is the real announcement. Wednesday’s had none.
