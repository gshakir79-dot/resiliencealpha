Brent Priced a Truce That Shipping Has Not Signed

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 11, 2026 3 min read

Brent rose 52 cents, or 0.6 percent, to $92.01 a barrel by 0730 GMT on Wednesday, November 11, and West Texas Intermediate rose 16 cents to $86.20. Reuters, reporting from Singapore, called it a bounce from the previous session two month lows. I read it differently. Oil is holding above $90 on the hope of a de-escalation in the US and Israeli war on Iran, while the ships that have to move the oil are getting hit at a record pace. The full Reuters report is here.

The consensus trade

The consensus has one honest leg. US crude stocks rose by 9 million barrels in the week ended November 6, according to market sources citing American Petroleum Institute data reported by Reuters. June Goh, a senior analyst at Sparta Commodities quoted by Reuters, pointed to more crude flows out of the Middle East and said the tone in the market had turned more bearish. On Tuesday, Brent first fell by more than $3 a barrel after US officials downplayed the need for further military strikes against Iran. That is the truce trade: fewer strikes, more barrels, lower prices.

The count says otherwise

Now count ships, not statements. Thirty seven ships were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz and surrounding waterways in October, the highest monthly total since the start of the war, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy cited by Reuters. Ten more attacks have been recorded so far in November. On Sunday, a Navig8 tanker was filmed on fire near the coast of Oman, in video verified by Reuters, one of three tankers hit that day for using routes Iran calls unauthorised. On Tuesday, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency reported a tanker struck northeast of Lima in Oman. The same day, Iran Revolutionary Guards Navy said two tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines on the unauthorised route in the strait. Mines change the problem. A missile threat ends when a ceasefire is announced. A minefield does not.

Why a deal would not fix flows on day one

LSEG Oil Research made the point in a note dated November 10, and Reuters quoted it in full: even if a deal is struck, an increase in flows through the waterway would still depend on mine clearance, insurance costs and whether shipowners are willing to take the risk. LSEG added the line that should be taped to every trading desk this week: attacks have not slowed. Part of Tuesday price recovery came only after an Iranian security official said no talks were taking place between the two sides, which cut against the official calm from Washington on the same day. Russia offered a second reminder on Wednesday that this is a wider supply story: a regional governor said a drone attack set fire to the port area of Ust-Luga on the Baltic Sea and injured three workers, and industry sources told Reuters that Russian refining capacity has fallen by about 30 percent since late October after renewed Ukrainian drone attacks.

My position, and what to watch

My position: at $92, Brent is priced for a diplomatic outcome that the attack count, the mines and the insurance market have not confirmed. The bearish inventory build is real, and I am not arguing for a spike. I am arguing that the downside case now needs shipping to get safer, and this week shipping got more dangerous. Watch three markers. First, the next UKMTO reports from the strait: a clean week would do more for the truce trade than any official statement. Second, any confirmed mine clearance plan, because that is the physical precondition for normal flows. Third, the next US inventory print after the 9 million barrel build: a second build, with attacks still running, would tell me the market has chosen stocks over ships, and I would have to revisit the call. Until then, the ships win.
