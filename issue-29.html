600,000 H200s Are Waiting in Hong Kong

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 6, 2026 3 min read

Reuters reported on Thursday, November 5, that about 600,000 Nvidia (NVDA) H200 chips are stored in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to mainland China. Washington has licensed H200 sales to approved Chinese customers. Beijing has allowed small batches in during recent months, and one person told Reuters that several major Chinese internet companies have each obtained about 10,000 chips over the past few months. Beijing has not approved large volume imports.

Two gates, one warehouse

The stockpile sits between two governments that both claim to control the flow. The United States barred H200 exports to China before the Trump administration gave Nvidia permission last year to sell to approved customers. None of that moves a chip across the border at Shenzhen. The second decision belongs to Beijing, and Reuters linked the hold to a reluctance to increase reliance on United States technology. China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and its National Development and Reform Commission did not respond to Reuters. Nvidia, Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek and Tencent did not respond either. Silence from all five buyers and both ministries is its own data point: nobody wanted to explain a 600,000 chip queue.

The price inside the wall

Scarcity has a quote. One person familiar with the matter told Reuters that an eight card H200 server was offered in mainland China for more than 6 million yuan, about $900,000, by the end of October. That is about three times its price a year earlier. The H200 is not Nvidia's newest processor. Reuters noted that Chinese alternatives have yet to match its capabilities for training AI models, particularly when combined with Nvidia's software. Nvidia has already paid for the mismatch once. In its quarterly report for the period ended July 26, 2026, the company recorded a $0.4 billion charge for H200 excess inventory and purchase obligations during the first half of fiscal 2027. Inventory built for a market that two governments can each close carries a carrying cost, and Nvidia has booked it.

The other door is a login

On the same day, November 5, CNBC reported that Moonshot AI, the Beijing company behind the Kimi models, has discussed renting Nvidia Blackwell capacity from neocloud operators in Europe. Four people familiar with the talks spoke to CNBC. One described clusters of 8,000 or more GPUs, and one proposal would have Moonshot pay 30 percent to 50 percent more upfront than a non-Chinese customer. CNBC reported no signed lease, and Moonshot and Nvidia did not respond to its requests for comment. The White House said in July that Moonshot had accessed Blackwell GB300 systems through a data centre in Thailand. Blackwell chips are barred from direct export to China, but CNBC noted that current law does not give regulators broad authority over access through cloud services hosted overseas. The Financial Times reported on November 3 that Nvidia has told its data centre partners to sell at least 51 percent of their services to non-Chinese companies, a private rule doing work that public law has not yet done.

What I am pricing

My position: a United States export licence for Nvidia into China is worth less than the headline suggests, because the binding approval sits in Beijing. Count H200 revenue when mainland approval arrives at volume. The Blackwell side points the other way. Until Congress acts, the control that matters for remote access is a contract term set by Nvidia itself. The Remote Access Security Act, which would extend export controls to cloud access, passed the House on January 12, 2026, by 369 votes to 22, and still sits in the Senate Banking Committee. Watch two markers: any Beijing approval that moves the Hong Kong stockpile in volume rather than in 10,000 chip batches, and the Senate calendar before year end.
