The Metal That Costs $9 in China
and $1,150 Outside It

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 22, 2026 3 min read

On October 21, Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning defended Beijing's rare earth curbs on Japan in public, saying the controls were meant to stop Japan's remilitarization. A day earlier, the Financial Times reported that Japan is months from running out of yttrium. Put those two facts together and you have the actual story. This is no longer a threat held in reserve. It is rationing, with a date attached.

What happened

Yttrium is used in thermal barrier coatings for jet engines, and in coatings inside the plasma etching chambers used to make semiconductors. Chinese customs data show China exported 14.1 tonnes of yttrium oxide to Japan in January to May 2026, down 97.7 percent from 605.3 tonnes a year earlier. In September, Japan received 4 tonnes of the 110 tonnes China exported that month. Austria, the United States, South Korea, Finland and Germany all received more. Japanese officials and trading house executives told the Financial Times that shortages could emerge as early as the end of this year.

One Japanese government official gave the quote that should end the argument about timing: "Stockpiles are decreasing and rare earth saving efforts have a limit. The timeline is not years, it is months and how to survive next year." A June survey of 400 Japanese procurement managers found 67.6 percent said yttrium was hard to obtain, and 17.5 percent reported production stoppages linked to rare earth shortages.

The one number

Argus puts yttrium oxide at $1,150 a kilogram outside China and $9 a kilogram inside China. That is a gap of about 128 to 1, and a 179 fold rise outside China in two years. A price gap that wide tells you the market has split into two systems: one with supply, and one bidding for leftovers. A senior United States official said in September that China was meeting about two thirds of its rare earth commitments to the United States, while Japan was getting only the bare minimum it needs. Washington has told Beijing it will not export the jet engines China needs for the Comac C919, citing yttrium constrained production, and China's May agreement to buy 200 Boeing jets still has not been completed, partly over critical minerals.

Why the alternative arrives late

My position is simple. Price the shortage, not the press release. The usual reply is that supply outside China is coming. For yttrium, the dates do not work. Lynas Rare Earths agreed in March to make 75 percent of its heavy rare earth oxides available to Japanese industry until 2038, through the Sojitz and JOGMEC joint venture called JARE, and yttrium is being added to that list. Lynas does not expect its first yttrium production until early 2028. Japan's seabed trial near Minamitorishima is planned for February 2027, with an economic assessment due by March 2028. Both routes are real. Neither one covers a shortage that Japanese officials date to the end of 2026.

Watch for a specific line in any United States and China deal in the next two months: does it name yttrium, or does it talk about rare earths in general. General language left Japan on the bare minimum after the May 17 White House fact sheet promised action on yttrium by name. If December arrives with no named yttrium commitment and no visible rise in Japanese imports, I would treat Lynas as a strategic asset with a late product, and Japanese engine and chip tool suppliers as the first place this shortage shows up in earnings.
