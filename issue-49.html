Issue #49 Free Article Silicon Sovereignty

$6.6 Billion Is Paused at the Licence Window

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 26, 2026 3 min read

Bloomberg reported on November 25, 2026 that lenders have delayed an upsized $6.6 billion loan sought by Bain Capital's Bridge Data Centres, whose Malaysian facilities are used by clients including ByteDance. The loan was near the finish line. Then the bankers read the new American export rule a second time, and the finish line moved.

What is actually paused

Bridge is the largest name on the list. One bank has decided to pass on a fully underwritten $372 million loan for Digital Halo, backed by Partners Group, for a data centre in Malaysia that services Alibaba and other customers. Multiple banks are reassessing a $480 million loan for Aolani, a Singapore based neocloud that gets business from ByteDance. At least one fund has decided not to participate in a $300 million loan to GMI Cloud, an Nvidia partner, to fund chips for a data centre in Thailand that Tencent would use. Add the four deals and $7.75 billion of financing is delayed, dropped or under review in one week.

The review is wider than four borrowers. Lenders including Citigroup and Standard Chartered, plus Singapore's major banks, have launched a review of data centre clients across the region, according to the same reporting. Some banks are trying to reduce exposure they already hold, through secondary loan sales and significant risk transfers, and are struggling to find buyers. Loans to data centre companies in Asia Pacific have risen from near zero to more than $49 billion since 2023. A large share of that debt was underwritten on contracts with Chinese technology customers.

The rule underneath it

The trigger is the Bureau of Industry and Security interim final rule of November 16, 2026, effective on Federal Register publication on November 19. Companies in Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil now need a United States licence to buy advanced AI chips. Separately, a data centre anywhere that leases significant compute to companies headquartered in China or another Country Group D:5 destination needs its own licence to keep doing so, and existing arrangements have 90 days to get one. That window closes around February 17, 2027. On November 26 the Financial Times reported that Nvidia has halted server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia while the licensing picture settles.

One detail in the Bloomberg reporting tells you how bankers are thinking. On the GMI Cloud loan, a proposed covenant would require the borrower to repay the entire loan early if Tencent were added to a United States restricted party list. Read that again. A sanctions listing, an event no borrower controls, is being written into loan documents as a repayment trigger. The banks are not waiting to see whether BIS grants licences. They are pricing the refusal in advance.

My read

My position: the export rule now travels through credit, and credit moves faster than licensing. A chip shipment can wait in a warehouse. A syndicated loan has signing dates, commitment fees and buyers who can walk. BIS has set a 90 day clock for licences and published no timeline for decisions. Every week of silence reprices $49 billion of regional debt a little further, whether or not a single licence is ever denied.

Watch February 17, 2027 first, and watch whether any BIS licence decisions land before it. Watch the Bridge syndication: if the $6.6 billion returns at a higher price with new covenants, the market has found its premium for this risk. If it returns smaller, or not at all, Southeast Asia's data centre buildout has a funding problem that no chip licence alone will fix.
