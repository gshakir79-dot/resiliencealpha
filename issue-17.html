The 2 Percent Gas Cut a Prime Minister
Answered on a Sunday

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 25, 2026 5 min read

Singapore's Energy Market Authority wants you to look at one number: less than 2 percent. Indonesia cut 31 million standard cubic feet a day of pipeline gas to Singapore starting Saturday, October 24, and the EMA says the reduction totals 17 billion British thermal units a day against a daily demand above 1,000. On Sunday, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong addressed his ruling party and talked about commercial contracts, LNG terminals, and nuclear power. Prime ministers do not spend a Sunday speech on a rounding error.

What happened

Djoko Siswanto, the chairman of upstream regulator SKK Migas, told Reuters on Saturday that Indonesia cut 15 million cubic feet a day from Natuna and another 16 million from Sumatra, from the Jambi Merang field. The gas is being diverted to domestic power: the Natuna volumes to a state utility unit in Batam, the Sumatra volumes toward electricity needs in Batam, Bintan, and Sumatra, Siswanto told Bloomberg in a text message the same day. He also pointed out a fact that explains the politics of the move. Asked whether other countries were affected, he said the pipelines from those fields go only to Singapore.

Jakarta has a domestic reason, and it is a good one. Earlier this month Batam, a data center and tourism hub across the strait from Singapore, sat through a 16-hour blackout that hit more than 411,000 customers, Bloomberg reported. The government replaced the chief executive of state electricity company PT Perusahaan Listrik Negara after the outage. A regulator choosing between a dark Batam and a contract with Singapore will choose Batam every time, and any honest reading of this story starts there.

On the Singapore side, the effects arrived the same day. Gas Supply Pte, one of the two importers of Indonesian pipeline gas, said in an email on Saturday that delivery of gas to its customers had already been affected by the reduction from its upstream supplier. Sembcorp Gas, which manages the receiving facility for the Natuna gas, did not comment outside business hours. The EMA, late Saturday, said Singapore GasCo stands ready to import more LNG at any time, and that importers will pursue the recourse available in their contracts.

The consensus take, and why the denominator is wrong

The calm case is stronger than most calm cases. The EMA noted that in recent months Singapore absorbed supply reductions of 20 to 100 billion British thermal units a day from planned and unplanned maintenance without disruption, by switching to other gas sources. This cut, at 17, sits below that range. Singapore generates about 95 percent of its electricity from natural gas, but it buys that gas from Indonesian and Malaysian pipelines and from LNG cargoes sourced worldwide. On volume alone, the EMA is right. The lights stay on.

Volume is the wrong test. Bloomberg, using energy ministry data compiled from SKK Migas reports, calculated that the cut equals about 14 percent of Indonesia's average pipeline gas exports to Singapore last year. The two supply contracts, one held through Gas Supply and the West Natuna agreement tied to Sembcorp Gas, are estimated to run until 2028. And the cut did not arrive as a quiet operational notice. On Thursday, October 22, after President Prabowo Subianto told his cabinet that Indonesia would safeguard its natural resources and put domestic needs first, Coordinating Minister Bahlil Lahadalia told reporters he might consider not extending gas exports to neighboring countries, adding: "we are in survival mode and must prioritize meeting our own domestic needs." Two days later, contracted gas stopped flowing at full rate.

That sequence is what Wong answered on Sunday. "That gas supply is protected by commercial contracts. We expect all parties to honor their commitments," he said, as reported by CNA. He added that Singapore's importers would take the steps needed to protect their rights under those contracts. Read the two statements side by side and the dispute is plain. Jakarta is talking about sovereignty and survival. Singapore is talking about paper. Both are talking about the same 31 million cubic feet.

My position: Singapore will pay for replacement LNG, probably through GasCo, and nobody in Singapore will notice a flicker. The cost lands in the spread between contracted pipeline gas and spot LNG, and Sembcorp Industries (U96) sits on the receiving end of the Natuna leg of that trade. Its 2023 annual report recorded a West Natuna gas purchase agreement valued at about S$1.9 billion. The real repricing, though, is 2028. Every pipeline contract in Southeast Asia that comes up for renewal after this weekend gets negotiated with a new clause in the room: the exporter kept a domestic override, used it mid-contract, and said so in the language of survival. Buyers will pay for firmness they used to assume. That is a transfer from importers to exporters, and it started with a cut the regulator priced at 2 percent.

What to watch

First, the clock. The EMA puts the reduction at 18 days, which runs to about November 10. If full volumes return on schedule, this was a Batam emergency measure and the contract argument stays theoretical. If the cut is extended, or a second one follows, the renewal repricing above is already under way. Second, the recourse: watch whether Gas Supply or Sembcorp Gas take a formal step under the contracts, which would turn a diplomatic exchange into a documented claim. Third, Bahlil's promised review of Indonesia's gas allocation, currently about 70 percent domestic and 30 percent exported, with downstream petrochemical feedstock next in line for the domestic share. Wong, for his part, used the same Sunday speech to say Singapore will work with the International Atomic Energy Agency on a nuclear infrastructure review starting in 2027. Countries do not queue up nuclear reviews because of 2 percent. They queue them up when the contract under the current system stops feeling like a guarantee.
