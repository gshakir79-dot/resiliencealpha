The $200,000 Cap Is the Real Stinger Competition

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 23, 2026 4 min read

On November 20, 2026, the US Army Contracting Command at Redstone Arsenal, Alabama opened a competition for a third maker of the Next Generation Short Range Interceptor, the missile meant to replace the FIM-92 Stinger. Lockheed Martin and RTX’s Raytheon have held the competing development contracts since March 2023. The Army now wants an alternate supplier alongside them, and it published the terms in plain numbers: proposals due December 20, 2026, unit prices below $200,000 for missiles and for launchers, and capacity for at least 11,000 interceptors and 2,200 launchers over ten years. Army Recognition reported the solicitation on November 23.

The consensus read, and why I disagree

The easy read is that a third seat means more competition, lower prices, and a real opening for a smaller maker. I read the document the other way. The $200,000 cap is only one filter. The schedule is the stricter one. Whoever bids must show up at live-fire demonstrations in May and June 2027 with working hardware, then deliver at least 50 developmental test missiles and six launchers within three months of those trials. Low-rate initial production has to be ready by September 2028. That sequence rewards a company that already has a seeker, a motor, and a factory. It punishes a company with a good slide deck and a plan to build all three.

Do the arithmetic the Army published. Eleven thousand missiles at the $200,000 ceiling is $2.2 billion of missile work before launchers, logistics, training, or support. Add 2,200 launchers at the same ceiling and the ten-year scenario reaches $2.64 billion. Averaged out, the winner has to sustain about 1,100 missiles a year, every year, at a fixed price the Army set before the first prototype award, which is planned for September 2027 under 10 U.S.C. 4022. A fixed price on a missile that has not yet passed its trials is a risk transfer, and the Army knows exactly who can absorb it.

What the missile has to do

The Stinger entered service in 1981 and reaches about 4.8 km on the SGT Stout vehicle. The replacement has to intercept helicopters, fixed-wing aircraft, and Group 2 and Group 3 drones out to 8 km, fired from the launchers the force already owns: the soldier-portable launcher, the Stryker-based SGT Stout, and the Marine Corps MADIS system. The three mandatory trials are specific enough to read like a warning. One shot at a Hot Plate thermal target suspended at least 25 meters above the ground at 6,000 meters. One shot at a Group 3 drone between 5,000 and 8,000 meters, head on. One shot at the same drone on a 90-degree crossing angle, where the target moves sideways while the missile flies. Pass or fail, no partial credit, and at least one missile in the set has to carry a live warhead.

That crossing shot is the human texture in this filing. A drone crossing at distance gives a weak infrared signature and keeps moving while the seeker tracks. Lockheed Martin says its QuadStar interceptor, built from its Miniature Hit-to-Kill work, has flown at White Sands Missile Range on January 13, 2026, and the company showed a full-scale final assembly line at Grand Prairie, Texas in April 2026. Raytheon announced a ballistic flight test on February 2, 2026 and guided firings at Dugway Proving Ground, Utah on July 15, 2026. Both incumbents are already spending their own money on the exact tests the new bidder has six months to prepare for.

My read

My position: the third seat is real, and it will probably stay empty of surprises. Foreign makers can bid with a qualified US partner, and non-traditional contractors and small businesses can qualify under the prototype rules, so the door is open on paper. The calendar closes it in practice. A bidder without a flying prototype today cannot build one, qualify it for SECRET-level work, and stand on the range in May with a straight face. The realistic contest is between the two incumbents and one or two well-funded challengers who have been working quietly before November 20. If a name outside that circle wins a prototype award in September 2027, I will treat it as the most interesting defense story of that year. I do not expect to write it.

For Lockheed Martin (NYSE: LMT), the solicitation is still useful information. The Army has now priced the Stinger replacement in public, capped it, and dated it. Any LMT or Raytheon win from here is a win inside a $200,000 box, which tells you where the margin pressure will land: on seekers, motors, and production rate, in that order. Watch December 20, 2026 first, when proposals close and the bidder list starts to leak. Watch the May to June 2027 range dates second. The third marker is the September 2027 prototype award itself: how many names are on it will tell you whether this competition ever had a third runner.
