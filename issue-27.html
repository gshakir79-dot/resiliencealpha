Issue #27 Free Article Silicon Sovereignty

Exempt on Paper, Limit-Down Twice in Taipei

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 4, 2026 3 min read

On Monday, November 2, the Legislative Yuan's Economics Committee held a public hearing in Taipei on a Foreign Trade Act amendment proposed by ruling Democratic Progressive Party lawmakers. It would ban exports of specified integrated circuits, key wafer materials, components and chipmaking equipment to mainland China without government approval. Violators would face up to 10 years in prison and fines of NT$5 million to NT$10 million, about US$155,000 to US$310,000. Routing through a third country carries the same penalties, and illegal gains would be confiscated.

The case that exposed the gap

The bill exists because prosecutors in Keelung walked through a hole in the current law in August. They indicted nine people, including Flying Tiger Technology's general manager and staff at the local units of Nvidia and Super Micro Computer, over 74 Nvidia B300 servers allegedly diverted to Chinese buyers for a profit above NT$600 million, about US$18.5 million. None of the nine faces an export charge, because selling the servers to China broke company rules and U.S. export curbs, not Taiwanese law. Their cover story was a rented data center in Taiwan. Prosecutors charged breach of trust and document forgery instead. Taipei was prosecuting a paperwork fraud, not an export violation.

William Liu, who runs the ministry's International Trade Administration, told the hearing that export controls "have shifted from preventing weapons proliferation to becoming a strategic tool for countries to protect their technological edge and national security." The direction is settled. The instrument is not. The ministry can designate controlled goods by public notice, the route Economy Minister Kung Ming-hsin outlined last month when he said AI servers and cutting-edge semiconductors would join the control list, with penalties up to five years. The bill doubles that to ten and makes confiscation mandatory. It also needs votes: the Kuomintang and the Taiwan People's Party together hold a Legislative Yuan majority, and neither has stated a position.

The stock that was not on the list

While Taipei debated chips, the market sold machinery. The ministry is studying a broader package, tied to commitments under the U.S.-Taiwan Agreement on Reciprocal Trade, and machine tools came up during the policy explanation stage. Officials then clarified that the first wave of new controls does not include machine tools or general machinery and equipment. Syntec Technology (7750), a maker of machine tool controllers with a high China revenue share, hit the daily limit-down on November 3 and again on November 4. Tongtai (4526), Goodway (1583) and Awea (1530) weakened alongside it.

Syntec's own answer was specific. Its main exports are controllers and servo motors, not complete machine tools. In China it supplies control systems and key components, so it says the direct impact is limited, and even a formal ban would have a relatively limited effect on overall operations. It kept its optimistic view on growth next year. That statement concedes the real question: whether a controller counts as a component of a controlled machine, or as a product of its own.

What I am pricing

My position: two limit-down sessions is a price on classification risk, not on lost orders. The risk worth owning is drift. Control lists tend to move from finished goods toward the parts that make those goods precise, and a controller is exactly that part in a machine tool. If a second wave reaches components, Syntec's exemption argument gets tested in law, not in a press statement.

Watch two dates. On November 13, Syntec holds an investor conference; its China revenue share and export control assessment are the numbers that matter. Before that, watch whether the ministry publishes its control list by notice. If controllers appear on a published list, I will revisit this. If they do not, Taipei will have charged the machinery chain a two-day toll for a law aimed at someone else.
