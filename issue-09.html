Washington Is Courting the
Wrong Address in Myanmar

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 17, 2026 4 min read

Reuters reported on October 16 that the United States and Myanmar's military-backed government held talks in Tokyo last week, their second meeting in a month. Assistant Secretary of State Michael Vance led the American side again, with more than a dozen US officials in the room. Three people familiar with the meeting spoke to Reuters about it. In mid-September, Vance sat across from coup leader turned president Min Aung Hlaing in Naypyidaw, the highest level US contact with Myanmar's generals since the 2021 takeover.

What happened

The reported agenda starts with an American businessman held in Myanmar and the scam centers that have swindled Americans out of billions of dollars. People familiar with the September talks told Reuters those meetings could open the way to broader discussions, including access to Myanmar's deposits of heavy rare earths, the dysprosium and terbium that go into EV motors, wind turbines and precision weapons. Hunter Marston, Southeast Asia director at the Lowy Institute, told Reuters Washington views Myanmar through a "purely transactionalist lens" and that rare earths are "probably the chief strategic asset that Washington is interested in within Myanmar."

The tell came last week, away from the meeting table. Washington lifted sanctions on the daughter of a Myanmar businessman accused of supporting the military. The State Department called it part of a new approach and said the move "highlights the importance of pursuing dialogue with all groups involved in Burma's civil war." Japan, which hosted the talks and hosted an earlier round in June, answered questions about its role with an emailed statement saying it had been "communicating as necessary" with both sides. Tokyo is saying as little as possible. Washington is spending sanctions relief. Both facts point the same way: the minerals are the meeting.

The consensus take is wrong

The consensus reading is that re-engagement opens a second source of heavy rare earths outside China, and that supply diversification is finally moving. I read the same Reuters story and land in the opposite place. US officials quoted in that reporting acknowledge the problem themselves: the deposits sit largely in territory controlled by ethnic armed groups fighting the military. The Kachin Independence Army seized the main mining belt, including the Chipwe and Pangwa area, in October 2024. The generals in the Tokyo room do not hold the ground the ore comes out of, and a signature from Naypyidaw cannot load a single truck.

Suppose the junta signs anyway. Kachin State is landlocked, mountainous, and wired by road and habit to one buyer: China, which also runs the separation plants the ore feeds. A person familiar with the discussions told Reuters that building a new supply chain from Kachin through India may simply not be feasible, because the infrastructure for it does not exist. Ore that cannot leave except through China, to be processed in China, is Chinese supply with extra paperwork.

My position: own the separator, not the summit. MP Materials (MP) collects the scarcity value whichever way the diplomacy breaks, because every non-China plan for heavy rare earths still ends at a separation plant, and MP is expanding heavy rare earth separation at Mountain Pass in California under a Pentagon package built for exactly this failure mode. In July 2025 the Defense Department bought $400 million of MP preferred stock and set a ten year price floor of $110 per kilogram on the company's NdPr oxide. The honest answer is that nobody knows whether the KIA ever takes a meeting with Washington. Until it does, Myanmar is a price story. Volume is not on offer.

The one number

52.8 percent. That is Myanmar's share of China's rare earth imports, according to S&P Global, and Kachin accounts for nearly half of the world's mined supply of heavy rare earths. Myanmar's exports to China peaked at nearly 42,000 metric tons in 2023, per CSIS. Washington is negotiating, with a government that lost the mining belt two years ago, for access to a flow that today runs almost entirely into the country it is trying to route around.

What to watch

Watch the guest list, not the communique. A round of talks that includes the Kachin Independence Army, or anyone who actually controls Chipwe and Pangwa, would change the math. Another round with only the junta changes nothing but the sanctions list, so watch OFAC delistings too: relief granted without ore attached is the price of the courtship, paid in advance. The hard date is November 10, 2026, when China's one year suspension of its expanded October 2025 rare earth export controls expires. If Beijing lets it lapse, heavy rare earth tightness stops being a Myanmar story and becomes a world price story, and the separator outside China gets more valuable either way.
