Issue #5 Free Article Energy Leverage

The IMF Just Normalized
$95 Oil

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 13, 2026 4 min read

On Tuesday in Bangkok, where finance ministers had gathered for the fall meetings, the International Monetary Fund published its October World Economic Outlook and raised world growth for 2026 to 3.1%, up from 3.0% in July. Buried under that upgrade was the assumption doing the real work: oil averaging $95.74 a barrel in 2026, up 41% from $67.74 in 2025, with natural gas up 48%. The Fund still called the year manageable. I think the Fund just told you $95 oil is the baseline, and most portfolios are still pricing it as a spike.

The consensus take

The read on the trading desks will be simple. Growth went up. The United States held at 2.3% for 2026 and 2027. Global inflation rises to 4.7% in 2026 and falls back to 4.1% in 2027 in the Fund forecast. Oil eases to $87.39 in 2027. On that telling, the energy shock hurt, the world absorbed it, and artificial intelligence spending covered the bill. The numbers were reported Tuesday from the WEO release. The headline writers will move on by Thursday.

Why that read is wrong

Start with who is doing the growing. The Fund said 32 economies now account for 95% of global growth, down from 54 in its April forecast, and that roughly 47% of economies had their forecasts cut. That is concentration, not breadth. Chief Economist Silvana Tenreyro wrote that the forecast stayed broadly unchanged despite a more persistent energy disruption than expected. Translated: a narrow set of AI heavy economies pulled the average up while the median economy got worse.

The Fund said so itself, in plainer language, about the losers. Energy importing countries with little stake in the technology boom face, in its words, the most difficult configuration. India is the exception that proves the split: growth raised to 7.0%, with consumer inflation projected at 5.0% and a wider current account deficit at 1.5% of GDP. An importer can grow and still pay the tax on every barrel.

Then read the assumption under the $95.74. The baseline expects shipping through the Strait of Hormuz to reopen by March 2027 and return to normal by October 2027. The Fund also warned that oil inventories are being drawn down toward stress levels that could trigger sharp price jumps, and it modelled an adverse case with oil near $130 a barrel into early 2027. The $87.39 figure for 2027 depends on a reopening arriving on schedule.

The one number that matters

The Fund ran the counterfactual for you. Without flexible supply responses, rerouted Gulf output, higher production elsewhere, and inventory drawdowns, the Hormuz supply loss would have put oil near $110 a barrel for the year and cut 0.9 percentage points from global growth. With those responses, the net hit fell to 0.3 points. Fine. Inventories, though, are a finite response. You can draw a stockpile once. The report credited a 20% fall in the oil intensity of world GDP since the mid 2010s for softening the blow, and that part is durable. The drawdown part is a clock.

My position: own the cash flows that collect the $95, not the ones that pay it and hope for $87. Integrated producers with low lifting costs and refining exposure, Exxon Mobil (XOM) at the front of that list, get paid under the baseline and get paid more under the adverse case. Import exposed airlines, shippers, and chemical margins get the reverse payout in both cases. The honest answer on timing is that nobody knows whether Hormuz reopens in March. The Fund has now admitted its own baseline depends on it.

What to watch

Inventory data comes first, because the Fund flagged the level alongside the price. Any slip in the March 2027 Hormuz assumption in Fund and agency updates matters too, since the 2027 oil price is built on it. US inflation on Wednesday, October 14 is the near test: if energy passes through faster than the 4.7% path implies, the central bank room that importers are counting on gets smaller. I am not waiting for oil to fall to call this regime what it is. At $95.74 assumed, $130 modelled, and inventories thinning, the burden of proof now sits with anyone still calling this transitory.
