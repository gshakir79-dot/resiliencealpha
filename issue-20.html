A Startup Got the F-16 on the Day Lockheed Got $694 Million

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 28, 2026 4 min read

On October 26, the Department of the Air Force announced a $694 million contract for Lockheed Martin (NYSE: LMT) to begin production of the AGM-183A ARRW, its first hypersonic weapon to reach that stage. The contract had been awarded on September 29. The same day, Castelion, a venture-backed startup founded in 2022, announced a contract modification from the Air Force Research Laboratory to begin integrating its Blackbeard hypersonic missile on the F-16. Most coverage treated these as two stories. They are one story, and the second half is the one that matters.

The consensus version, and where it stops

The ARRW half got the headlines, and the revival angle writes itself. The Air Force moved to end the program in 2023 after a run of failed flight tests. On Monday, Secretary of the Air Force Troy Meink called the production decision "a turning point in our advanced weapons roadmap" and said it gives combatant commanders "a credible, high-speed strike weapon capable of penetrating the most advanced anti-access and area-denial systems." Chief of Staff Gen. Ken Wilsbach said fielding ARRW lets the joint force hold targets at risk "anywhere around the world." Fine. All of that is in the release. What the release withholds, citing operational security, is the only part an investor can price: how many missiles $694 million buys, and when they arrive.

Blackbeard sits at the opposite end of the cost curve. Air & Space Forces Magazine, which reported both announcements on October 27, describes a rocket-powered boost-glide weapon with a range of nearly 500 miles, sized at roughly an AIM-120 AMRAAM. The Navy ran carriage and separation tests from an F/A-18 Super Hornet on September 18 at Naval Air Station Patuxent River, Maryland. The Navy picked Blackbeard as the winner of its Multimission Affordable Capacity Effector program, a program of record with framework orders of up to $200 million. Castelion won a $105 million contract in April to integrate the missile on the Super Hornet, and a $23.4 million contract in June for 50 pre-production missiles. The Air Force has more than 600 F-16s in its fleet. A cheap hypersonic round that fits a fighter the service already owns in bulk is a magazine-depth argument, and magazine depth is where the United States is thinnest.

Read the money, in order

Here is the discipline this page applies to every defense announcement. Integration money is an option. Production money is a commitment. On the Air Force side, Blackbeard is still option money: a $16.4 million modification added in September, plus the F-16 work announced Monday, with no value and no timeline disclosed for the new award. Castelion's own announcement was eleven words of substance on X, ending with "Aim High." I like the confidence. I cannot price it, and neither can you. The honest answer on Blackbeard unit cost is that nobody outside the program knows yet.

My position: the Air Force is hedging on purpose, because it has to. ARRW gives bombers a long-range weapon now, carried first by the B-52, with a fiscal 2027 budget request that seeks $296 million for an Increment 2 version with a terminal seeker and a data link, so it can hit moving targets, including ships. Blackbeard, HACM from Raytheon (NYSE: RTX) and Northrop Grumman (NYSE: NOC), and the rest of the portfolio exist because a force built only from exquisite rounds runs out of rounds. The services learned that arithmetic from watching interceptor stocks in Ukraine and the Red Sea. Monday's pairing is the Pentagon admitting, in contract form, that unit cost is now a performance specification.

What to watch

Two markers, both with dates attached. First, congressional appropriations for fiscal 2027: the $296 million Increment 2 request either survives or it does not, and that vote tells you whether ARRW production is a program or a one-off buy. Second, the Navy's next Blackbeard delivery order under that $200 million framework. A production order for Castelion, in disclosed numbers, converts Monday's integration news from venture storytelling into an industrial fact. Until one of those prints, I am treating the $694 million as real revenue with unknown timing for Lockheed, and the F-16 headline as the cheapest option the Air Force bought all year.
