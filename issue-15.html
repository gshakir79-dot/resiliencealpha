Qatar Is Now Buying the Gas
It Used to Sell

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 23, 2026 4 min read

On October 22, Cheniere Energy announced two sale and purchase agreements with QatarEnergy: 2 million tonnes a year from Sabine Pass in Louisiana, 2 million tonnes a year from Corpus Christi in Texas, free on board, priced on Henry Hub plus a fixed fee, running through the end of 2054. QatarEnergy is one of the largest LNG sellers on earth. Sellers with spare supply do not sign 23 year purchase contracts in someone else's hemisphere. Buyers with a hole to fill do.

The consensus version

Both companies sold the deal as growth. QatarEnergy chief executive Saad Sherida Al-Kaabi, who is also Qatar's minister of state for energy affairs, said the purchase would "expand and diversify our global LNG portfolio" and "complement QatarEnergy's existing LNG supplies." Cheniere chief executive Jack Fusco called the agreements a "major long-term commercial commitment" and said they reinforce Cheniere's position as the leader in U.S. LNG. That language is tidy, quotable, and incomplete. Portfolio managers diversify at the margin, in cargoes and seasons. They do not lock in supply to 2054 to round out a trading book.

What Ras Laffan explains

Start with the damage Qatar does not put in this press release. Iranian strikes in March hit Qatar's production hub at Ras Laffan Industrial City. In a March 19 interview with Reuters, Al-Kaabi said two of the country's 14 LNG trains had been hit, and that repairs would keep 12.8 million tonnes a year offline for three to five years. That is 17 percent of Qatar's LNG export capacity. He also said QatarEnergy would have to declare force majeure for up to five years on long-term contracts supplying buyers in Italy, Belgium, South Korea and China from the two damaged trains, where ExxonMobil is a partner. Read the Cheniere contracts against that paragraph and the shape changes. A cargo loaded at Sabine Pass or Corpus Christi reaches a buyer in Europe or Asia without crossing the Strait of Hormuz, and without depending on a repair schedule in Ras Laffan.

The one number

Four million tonnes a year is the headline. Set it next to 12.8 million tonnes a year offline and it covers less than a third of the hole. Deliveries begin in the early 2030s on Cheniere's wording, in 2031 on QatarEnergy's. The repair window Al-Kaabi gave in March runs three to five years, so it closes between 2029 and 2031. The American supply starts roughly when the damaged trains are supposed to return. Either Qatar expects the repairs to slip, or it expects to keep selling volumes it cannot guarantee from home, or both. The honest answer is that nobody outside Ras Laffan knows the repair timeline with confidence, and Qatar just paid Henry Hub plus a fixed fee to stop depending on it.

One detail in Cheniere's release deserves more attention than it got. One million tonnes a year of the Sabine Pass volume, a quarter of the whole deal, is conditional on a positive final investment decision for the first phase of the Sabine Pass expansion. That phase is a single train, Train 7, plus a boil-off gas re-liquefaction unit, with expected capacity above 6 million tonnes a year. Bechtel signed the lump sum engineering and construction contract in May 2026 and is working under a limited notice to proceed. A customer of QatarEnergy's size, signing for a quarter century, is exactly the kind of anchor contract that turns a limited notice into a full investment decision.

My position: treat this as Cheniere selling reliability, and price it that way. Cheniere runs about 56 million tonnes a year of liquefaction capacity in operation, with about 5 million more under construction, and its shares closed up 0.98 percent at $287.01 on October 22, a muted reaction to a contract that runs into the second half of the century. The market logged a portfolio deal. I log something else: the strongest LNG seller in the Gulf concluded that Gulf supply, including its own, now carries a risk it has to hedge with American molecules. Watch two markers. First, the final investment decision on Sabine Pass Train 7, which this contract makes easier to justify. Second, any second U.S. purchase by QatarEnergy, which would confirm that 4 million tonnes was a floor, not a ceiling.

LNG · Cheniere Energy, Inc.
