Issue #44 Free Article Silicon Sovereignty

879,868 Licences, Zero Proven Shipments

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 21, 2026 4 min read

On Thursday, November 19, 2026, House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast released Commerce Department licensing records that Washington had kept private. The Bureau of Industry and Security approved 879,868 Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) H200 and AMD (NASDAQ: AMD) MI325X chips for buyers in China, or with a Chinese parent, across 21 applications as of May 11, 2026. Chinese buyers had asked for 3,444,160 chips. Mast released the records under the Export Control Reform Act after the committee decided withholding them was contrary to the national interest, calling it the most detailed public disclosure in the history of that law. The committee release is summarised by Technology.org and The Register.

The consensus take, and why I disagree

The headline version writes itself. Washington approved nearly 900,000 advanced chips for China, including firms the Pentagon lists as Chinese military companies, then spent the same week tightening the rules. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer said on Friday that President Trump and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had sold out national security. Representative Gregory Meeks, the committee’s top Democrat, said BIS had failed to enforce the rules. I read the same table and reach a different conclusion. An approval is a ceiling on permission. It is not a shipment, not revenue, and not a chip in a rack in Shenzhen.

The records themselves say so. As of May 11, 2026, none of the approved chips had shipped, according to the committee records reported on November 20. Nvidia told the Associated Press on Friday, November 20 that the limited shipments it made under the H200 program were legal and authorized, that approvals carried strict conditions and limited volumes, and that it has since stopped shipping H200s to China despite holding licences. Earlier this week Nvidia said the same thing in plain language: shipments have stopped. If you are pricing NVDA on the idea that 879,868 chips turned into China sales, you are pricing a number the source document does not support.

The part of the table that should bother you

Four names on the Pentagon’s 2026 list of Chinese military companies account for about 272,000 approved chips, nearly a third of the total. Tencent and its subsidiaries asked for 150,000 and received approval for all 150,000. Alibaba asked for 948,950 and received approval for 75,000. Baidu received 43,000 and BYD received 4,000. BIS also refused some large requests, including 610,000 chips for Alibaba and 508,000 for ByteDance, the TikTok owner, in separate applications reported by The Register. One AMD application, a proposed transfer of 75,000 chips to UCloud, was rejected.

Here is the human texture, and it sits in one line repeated across the file. Every Nvidia and AMD application stated that its proposed transfer posed a low risk of diversion. Every one. The committee disputes that rating and says some Nvidia applications listed possible system installers that public reports have questioned over diversion risk, including firms linked in reporting to Megaspeed. A self-graded low risk, filed by the seller, accepted in a case by case process that BIS says follows a January 2026 rule with added security conditions, is not a control system. It is paperwork with a signature on it.

What Bessent actually settled on Friday

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, speaking to CNBC on Friday, November 20 after returning from Asia with President Trump, said: “The proper chip restrictions are in place. And the remote access has been curtailed.” He said no further restrictions on sales of older Nvidia chips to China are needed. Read that against the week. On November 19 the BIS interim final rule on remote access took effect, the rule covered in Issue #41 of this publication. Reuters reported the same day that TSMC (NYSE: TSM) had suspended some orders from Chinese designers at 16 nanometres and below unless they obtain US export licences first. The binding constraint moved this week from the chip sale to the foundry order and the rented data centre. Bessent is defending the licence shelf while Commerce closes the routes around it.

My position: the investable risk here is process risk, not booked China revenue. Nvidia already guides on the basis of no China data centre compute revenue, per its recent reporting, and it has stopped H200 shipments. What can still move the stock is a congressional move to review or revoke licences, a hearing that forces Commerce to publish shipment counts rather than approval counts, and the licence queue now forming at TSMC and Samsung for Chinese designers who used to clear on specifications alone. Schumer’s attack and Meeks’s oversight call point the same way. The committee has already said it plans hearings next month with Lutnick and his deputy Jeff Kessler.

Watch three markers, in order. First, any Commerce publication of actual shipment numbers for the 879,868 approvals. That single number decides whether this was a scandal of deliveries or a scandal of paperwork, and nobody in Washington has published it yet. Second, the December hearings and any bill that gives Congress a review window over individual chip licences. Third, whether TSMC resumes the suspended Chinese orders under licence, and on what timeline. Until the shipment count prints, treat 879,868 as permission granted to firms Washington itself lists as military linked, and treat zero as the only shipment figure the records have actually proven.
