1,000 Tomahawks a Year, One Qualified Booster

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 19, 2026 4 min read

On Wednesday, November 18, 2026, Ursa Major and Avio USA announced a joint contract from Raytheon, an RTX business, to design, develop and qualify a new solid rocket motor booster for the Tomahawk cruise missile. The award creates, in the companies' own words, a pathway for both firms to become qualified United States suppliers for the program. The joint release is here. Read the verb. The contract buys qualification work. It does not buy a single finished booster.

The consensus read

Coverage on November 18 and 19 treated the award as capacity arriving: two new suppliers, a dual-sourced bottleneck, a supply chain being fixed in public. Raytheon encouraged that reading. Sam Deneke, president of Naval Power at Raytheon, said: “By dual-sourcing this critical component with both Avio USA and Ursa Major, we’re strengthening our supply chain, encouraging innovation, and building the capacity needed to meet the Navy’s mission requirements well into the future.” That last phrase is doing the heavy lifting in the sentence. Well into the future is where this award lives.

Read the release for what it omits

The joint release states no contract value, no production quantity, no qualification date and no delivery schedule. The companies say they intend to qualify the jointly designed booster and demonstrate eligibility for future production orders. One secondary report, Traders Union citing Axios, put the award above $80 million. The companies themselves did not, so I am working from their document, and their document is a development award with the money left blank.

Neither partner builds Tomahawk boosters today. The qualified booster is the Mk 135, built by L3Harris Technologies (LHX), which has supported the program for more than 30 years and also builds the missile's blast-fragmentation warhead. In September 2026 L3Harris signed a multi-year public-private partnership with the Naval Air Warfare Center Weapons Division at China Lake, California, to expand Tomahawk component capacity. Raytheon is adding sources alongside that incumbent. It is not replacing it, and nothing in the November 18 release changes who can ship a booster next quarter.

Avio USA's own capacity tells the same story in concrete. The company broke ground on September 29, 2026 on a plant of about 900,000 square feet at the Southern Virginia Multimodal Park in Hurt, Virginia, a $500 million site planned to produce thousands of tactical motors a year and employ about 1,500 people. Construction is expected to finish in late 2028, with production starting in early 2029. Its chief executive, James Syring, a retired Navy vice admiral who once ran the Missile Defense Agency, called the Tomahawk award “a significant step forward for our planned U.S. manufacturing facility.” Planned is the accurate word in that quote too.

The ramp this has to feed

The demand side is real, and it is already contracted. On August 17, 2026, the Navy awarded Raytheon a seven-year, $22.9 billion contract to raise Tomahawk output from roughly 60 missiles a year to more than 1,000, part of the Department of War's Arsenal of Freedom initiative after sustained combat expenditure in the Middle East. Reporting on that award put Tomahawk lead times at 18 to 24 months from order to delivery. The booster is a short-burn part with an outsized vote on that schedule: it drives the missile out of a Mk 41 vertical launch cell or a submarine tube and accelerates it until the wings deploy and the Williams International turbofan takes over, then it separates. Navy data gives Block IV and Block V missiles a range of about 1,600 km. A booster that has not passed static fire, environmental and aging tests cannot ride in that sequence, whatever the press release calls it.

My read: price the three names in this story differently. RTX owns the $22.9 billion ramp and the schedule risk that comes with it, starting now. L3Harris owns the only qualified booster line and the China Lake expansion, which makes it the near-term beneficiary of every missile Raytheon actually delivers. Ursa Major and Avio USA own an option on a second source. Options on defense programs can be worth a great deal, and Avio's Virginia plant plus Ursa Major's additive manufacturing work on other Navy motors, including a $10 million Mk 104 award in July 2026, make this a credible option. Credible and exercised are different states, and the distance between them is measured in qualification milestones, not announcements.

Watch for three markers, in order: a disclosed qualification schedule from Raytheon or the Navy, a static fire of the jointly designed booster, and a production order written separately from this development award. Until the first of those appears, the honest count of qualified Tomahawk booster suppliers stays at one, and the climb from 60 missiles a year to 1,000 still runs through L3Harris.

RTX · RTX Corporation
