Malaysia Opened the Rare Earth Door by 30 Tonnes

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 29, 2026 3 min read

Malaysia banned the export of raw rare earths in 2024. On October 27, Reuters reported that DTEC Mineral and Metal Technology chief executive Milan Perlly said his company has won the first permit under this month’s eased rules: up to 700 tonnes of rare earth carbonate from Perak state, with a first shipment of about 30 tonnes bound for the United States this weekend. Skillings Mining Review, summarising the same report on October 28, puts the departure from Penang on October 31.

What is confirmed, and what rests on one man’s word

Start with the paperwork, because the paperwork is where this story gets honest. Malaysia’s natural resources ministry did not respond to a Reuters request for comment. Bloomberg reported last week that the ministry had granted a single export application under the new rules and did not name the company. Shanghai Metals Market reports the ministry saying the Department of Minerals and Geoscience approved an application for the export of unprocessed rare earths for research and development, the first permit since the 2024 ban was relaxed, with the applicant’s identity not disclosed. So a permit exists. That DTEC holds it is, so far, Perlly’s statement in an interview. I am printing both halves of that sentence because readers deserve to know which half they are standing on.

The rules themselves are confirmed. The 2024 ban was meant to keep raw material at home and force processing into Malaysia. Shipments to China were still allowed under a pilot mining project in Perak. This month the government released guidelines for conditional exports of raw rare earths for research or processing, aimed at pulling in refining technology and Western buyers who want supply that does not run through China.

The one number, and the deposit behind it

Seven hundred tonnes of carbonate for separation research will not move a market. Malaysia’s own government estimate is 16.1 million tonnes of rare earth deposits, and Reuters notes the country lacks the technology to mine and process them at scale. That gap is the whole trade. The material is to go to United States companies and research institutions to test separation and refining of elements from Malaysia’s ionic clay deposits. DTEC is registered in Malaysia and 51 percent owned by United States citizens, including Malaysian-born Perlly. He told Reuters the firm aims to build a processing plant in Malaysia within three years: “Our purpose is to really develop Malaysian talent, know-how and an ecosystem of industries supporting rare earths expansion in the country.” He also said the Export-Import Bank of the United States has given an expression of support for potential financing of up to $650 million for that proposed refinery. EXIM did not respond to a request for comment, so treat the $650 million as a claim awaiting a signature. The United States embassy in Kuala Lumpur said on October 14 that more than a dozen United States companies are now exploring upstream and midstream projects in Malaysia.

What to watch

My position: buy the rule change, not the press release. A country that banned raw exports two years ago has now written a research exception into the ban, and Washington has a dozen firms walking the ground. That is a policy direction with dates on it. The first date is October 31, when the 30 tonnes are due to leave Penang. If they ship, the next marker is a ministry statement naming the permit holder, and after that an EXIM reply that turns an expression of support into financing terms. A test shipment is a question. A second permit is an answer.
