Viasat Won the Layer Between the Satellites

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 8, 2026 3 min read

Space Systems Command announced on Thursday, November 5 that Viasat, Inc. (VSAT) has won a competitive indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contract to build the Mission Operations Center for the Space Data Network Backbone. The Space Force runs the program with Golden Dome for America, the homeland missile defense initiative. Viasat will serve as the network and mission system integration partner. The satellites belong to other companies. The layer that decides where their data goes belongs to Viasat.

What the center does

The Backbone is a low Earth orbit data network meant to move sensor and targeting data between satellites, ground stations and weapons platforms. The operations center is its orchestration hub. It integrates constellations from multiple vendors, routes traffic by mission priority in real time, and shifts data from one constellation to another when a link slows from congestion or adversary jamming, according to Breaking Defense on November 6. Lt. Col. Alexa Eggert, the Space Force materiel leader for the program, put the job plainly: "The MOC establishes the mission operations layer needed to integrate the diverse space data transport and space exchange systems being developed across related efforts." The release did not say where the center will be built. DefenseScoop noted that omission on November 6. A center with no announced site has no announced schedule either.

Ceiling, order, cash: three different numbers

The headline number is a $450 million ceiling. The ordered number is a $115 million initial task order to design, field and scale the center. Those are different things, and most coverage of this award ran the ceiling as if it were revenue. The surrounding program shows the scale of the bet. SpaceX holds a $2.29 billion agreement to deliver a fully operational Backbone prototype by the end of 2027. Earlier delivery orders brought in Amazon LEO for Government, Lockheed Martin (LMT), Northrop Grumman (NOC), Rocket Lab (RKLB) and York Space Systems to demonstrate data transport across vendors. Every one of those vendors now works through an integration layer run by Viasat. That position is worth more than the first task order. It is also hostage to a deadline Viasat does not control: an operations center is only as early as the constellation it serves.

The stock has already voted, on thin evidence

Viasat reported fiscal second quarter results the same day, November 5. Revenue was $1.18 billion, up 3.4 percent from a year earlier and below the $1.20 billion consensus estimate. The net loss widened to $129 million. Inside the mix, Defense and Advanced Technologies revenue rose 20 percent to $365 million, while total awards for the quarter fell 24 percent to $1.1 billion. The shares closed Friday, November 6 at $63.95, up 7.7 percent on the day, for a market value of about $8.8 billion. Against $4.66 billion of revenue over the last twelve months, a $115 million task order is a rounding item. The market is paying for the seat, not the order.

My position, and what settles it

I think the seat is the right thing to pay for, within limits. Integrators collect rent from every vendor added later, and the Space Force has already said it wants more vendors, not fewer. The honest unknown is conversion: how much of the $450 million ceiling becomes task orders, and how fast. Watch two things. First, any follow-on task order beyond the initial $115 million, which would show the ceiling turning into work. Second, the end of 2027 SpaceX prototype milestone, because Viasat's layer has no traffic to route until that hardware flies. This is a position trade on program structure until the first lands. I am comfortable with that, sized like it.
