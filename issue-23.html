The $62 Billion Hill With No Proven Mine Yet

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research October 31, 2026 4 min read

On Friday, October 30, Kenya’s mining minister, Hassan Ali Joho, told Reuters that his government will name a developer for the Mrima Hill deposit within three weeks. Five consortia filed tenders on October 21 for the site in Kwale County, about 65 kilometres from the port of Mombasa. Two Chinese firms, Hunan Research Institute for Nonferrous Metals and Jinan Yuxiao Group, withdrew from the process. Joho put the timeline in his own words: “Within the next two, three weeks we should be able to award Mrima Hills to the successful bidder.” Preliminary estimates cited in Kenyan coverage put the rare earths and niobium in the ground at $62 billion. Read that number carefully. It is the gross value of metal in a hill, quoted before anyone has established that mining it pays.

What the bidders are actually buying

Three of the five remaining groups carry a US flag. Meridian Mineral Holdings includes Fluor Corp (NYSE: FLR) and Tetra Tech (NASDAQ: TTEK). Frontier Minerals includes ReElement Technologies. Mrima Earth is led by Critical Metals Corp (NASDAQ: CRML), the company also pursuing the Tanbreez rare earth project in Greenland. Australia’s RareX has teamed with Iluka Resources (ASX: ILU), and a Kenyan-British joint venture called Gem of Dreams rounds out the list. Joho said he did not know why the two Chinese firms pulled out, and he said Kenya faced no pressure from Beijing or Washington. I will take the second claim at face value when the winner is announced and the processing terms are published. Until then, the withdrawal is the most interesting fact in the story, because firms rarely walk away from a tender this public without a reason they prefer not to print.

The honest gap in the coverage sits in one sentence from the Reuters report: Kenya has yet to establish the project’s economic viability. There is no published feasibility study in the tender reporting, no reserve statement, and no disclosed figure for what local processing Kenya will require from the winner. President William Ruto said earlier this year that Nairobi was close to signing a critical minerals agreement with the United States, with local processing as part of the point. Niobium adds a second prize to the hill. The metal hardens steel and serves aerospace work, and production today sits primarily in Brazil and Canada. A third producing country would matter. A tender is not a producing country.

Three ways the next three weeks go

Scenario one, a US-linked winner, 50 percent. One of the three US-flagged consortia takes the award, Washington gets a supply chain headline it did not have to subsidize, and Nairobi ties the project to the pending US critical minerals agreement. This breaks if the winning bid carries no financing detail and no processing commitment. An award ceremony is cheap. A separation plant is not.

Scenario two, the date slips, 30 percent. Tenders closed only on October 21, the viability work is unfinished, and ministerial timelines in mining ministries have a long record of optimism. If no developer is named by November 21, treat the three-week promise as the first missed marker and reprice every later date in the project by the same delay. This breaks if Joho names a winner early, which would suggest the decision was effectively made before the tenders closed.

Scenario three, the outsiders win, 20 percent. RareX and Iluka, or Gem of Dreams, take the hill. That outcome would tell you Kenya ran an auction rather than an alignment exercise, and it would be the healthier signal for the project itself, because it would mean the bid was judged on money and technical plan. It breaks the Washington narrative, and it would send the US consortia looking for the next deposit on the continent with a lesson learned about assuming the flag does the work.

My position

For Fluor, this tender is an engineering fee opportunity long before it is a mining story, and engineering firms get paid for studies and construction whether or not the orebody performs. For Critical Metals, a win would be far more consequential and far more demanding, because a small developer that adds a Kenyan hill to a Greenland project has to fund both. I am not paying for the $62 billion figure in any of these names. I am watching two dated markers: the award announcement itself, due by November 21 on Joho’s own timeline, and the first document after it that carries a feasibility number, a processing commitment, or a financing line. The first document with a number in it is the real announcement. Friday’s had a calendar date and a hill. That is a start, and only a start.
