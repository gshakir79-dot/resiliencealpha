The 61-Day Truce

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 27, 2026 4 min read

On November 24, 2026, China’s Ministry of Commerce published Announcement No. 59 of 2026. It extends the suspension of the clause that in principle banned gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard material exports to the United States, from November 28, 2026 to January 27, 2027. The wires filed it as an extension of the truce. Read the dates before you file it that way. The first suspension, announced on November 9, 2025, ran about a year. This one runs 61 days.

The consensus read

The relief case writes itself. The extension is seamless: the old pause ends on November 27 and the new one begins the next morning, with no gap and no new conditions. Trade in four strategic material groups stays legally possible. Nobody has to explain a lapsed truce to a procurement board in December. If you stopped at the headline, you would call this de-escalation and move on.

I stopped at the operative sentence. Announcement No. 59 has one. It suspends the second clause of Announcement No. 46 of 2024 for a named window, and it does nothing else. The 2024 order itself was not withdrawn. A settlement removes the instrument. This keeps the instrument, prints a shorter expiry date on it, and sets the next negotiation for January.

What the announcement leaves standing

Start with the clause that never moved. The first clause of the 2024 order prohibits exports of dual-use items to United States military users, or for military use. It has never been suspended, and Announcement No. 59 does not touch it. Then the licensing system underneath everything: gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials and graphite all remain listed dual-use items, so every shipment still needs an export licence applied for and granted. The suspension removes a presumption of refusal. It creates no presumption of approval, and it promises no volumes, no quotas and no timelines.

The separate rare earth regime is untouched as well. Announcement No. 18 of April 4, 2025 requires an individual licence for every export of seven medium and heavy rare earths, including dysprosium, terbium and yttrium, plus finished permanent magnets. On November 26, ministry spokesperson He Yadong told the regular press briefing in Beijing that China administers the control lists according to statutory regulations, to uphold international non-proliferation standards and national security. That is the sound of a system describing itself as permanent. And the other suspended measure, the extraterritorial rule covering foreign products with as little as 0.1 percent Chinese rare earth content, was extended on November 9 only to January 10, 2027. Two cliffs, 17 days apart.

The numbers underneath

Licensing is not a formality, and the trade data says so. Average monthly United States imports of Chinese rare earth compounds and metals ran at 19.3 metric tons between November 2025 and September 2026, down 74.9 percent from 76.8 tons before the licensing regime. Yttrium imports fell from 333 metric tons across an eight-month baseline to 17 tons. Exporters file six sets of compliance documents per application, and processing routinely exceeds 45 days. The concentration behind those numbers is why a licence window matters at all: China accounts for 98 percent of primary refined gallium output and 79 percent of natural graphite output in the United States Geological Survey figures, and gallium and germanium are by-product metals, so supply cannot be switched on quickly when a window closes.

My read

My position: the length of the extension is the policy. A one-year suspension in November 2025 gave buyers a planning horizon. A 61-day suspension gives them a queue. The rational response is to file licence-supported orders now rather than in January, to price any delivery dated after January 27 differently from one inside the window, and to keep non-Chinese refining and recovery projects funded even while Chinese material is available. For holders of the Western supply chain, MP Materials (NYSE: MP) included, the relief headline cuts the urgency and changes nothing structural, because 61 days does not cancel a single qualification programme.

Watch January 10, 2027 first, when the 0.1 percent rule suspension lapses, then January 27, when this one does. If a further announcement lands well before those dates, Beijing is managing a calendar. If it lands at the deadline, or not at all, the calendar is the weapon and the 61 days were the tell.
