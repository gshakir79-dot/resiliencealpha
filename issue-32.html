Issue #32 Free Article Energy Leverage

500,000 Tonnes of Diesel Promised, Zero Discharged

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 9, 2026 4 min read

Al Jazeera reported on Saturday, November 7 that no Russian diesel had reached the United States under the deal Donald Trump announced with Vladimir Putin on October 9. The count, one month in, is exact. Four tracked tankers loaded diesel at Primorsk, Russia's Baltic export port. Two changed course away from the US East Coast. A third crossed the Atlantic and then deleted New York from its destination board. Confirmed US discharges: zero.

The consensus read, and why I am not buying it

The consensus read after October 9 was simple. The Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued General License 135 the same day as the announcement, authorizing transactions tied to imports of Russian origin diesel through April 7, 2027. More than 300,000 metric tons would move immediately, Reuters reported on October 21, with 500,000 metric tons promised for November, according to Steptoe's October 16 summary of the arrangement. Diesel sat at record levels, the trade was licensed, and so the diesel crack would soften once the ships arrived. Every step in that chain has failed at the same place: a counterparty who can still say no. The full tracking record is laid out in this tanker-by-tanker account of the stall.

The statute that outranks the licence

General License 135 waives Treasury's own sanctions regulations. It does not repeal the Ending Importation of Russian Oil Act of 2022, a statute Congress passed that bans Russian petroleum imports until the president certifies that Russia has withdrawn from Ukraine and hostilities have ended. No such certification exists. That gap is the whole story. On November 2, New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill wrote to a dozen port and terminal operators, including Kinder Morgan, IMTT and Phillips 66 (PSX), warning of serious civil and criminal consequences if they took the fuel, as local reporting on the letter detailed. Kinder Morgan replied that it had received no notice of a Russian diesel shipment to its terminal. Senator Jeanne Shaheen warned banks and ports off the cargoes, and a House bill to nullify the licence is already filed. A bank, an insurer and a terminal operator each read the same two documents and reach the safer answer. Kpler analyst Matt Smith said it plainly: regulatory and sanctions concerns could stop cargoes discharging on the US Atlantic coast.

Four ships, read off their own signals

Ship tracking is the one witness in this dispute with no press office. The Russian flagged Talisman loaded at Primorsk and declared the US East Coast; by November 5 it had entered the Mediterranean and was signalling "for orders" near Algeria, meaning it awaits instructions on where to discharge. Leruo, Sierra Leone flagged, switched its destination to Brazil. Agioi Fanendes, Greek owned and Malta flagged, loaded about 40,000 tonnes of ultra low sulphur diesel on October 22, listed New York with an arrival near November 7, then changed its board to "for orders" in mid Atlantic. Beast, a sanctioned Seychelles owned tanker, loaded on October 27 and signals only "Atlantic." Moscow disputes the record. Presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev said on November 6: "Fake. Tankers destined for the US continue on course." Deputy Foreign Minister Aleksandr Pankin told Reuters, in a report dated November 9, that the diesel agreement could serve as a foundation for restoring commercial relations, while conceding it remains unclear whether shipments can actually be carried out. That concession, from the selling side, is the most honest sentence in the file.

Too small to rescue the pump price anyway

Suppose one cargo finally discharges. The arithmetic still disappoints. US diesel averaged about $6.23 to $6.27 a gallon in early November, according to AAA figures cited in market coverage, and GasBuddy data cited by Reuters put diesel at a record $6.28 a gallon on election day, November 3. The Energy Information Administration put Central Atlantic on-highway diesel at $6.49 a gallon on November 2, up $2.58 from a year earlier. Against that shortage, Kpler analyst Michelle Brouhard estimates roughly 400,000 barrels a day of extra supply is needed to ease prices materially. The entire November promise, 500,000 metric tons, converts to roughly 3.7 million barrels, a one-off volume equal to about nine days of the shortfall Brouhard describes. Ukraine says it is monitoring eight tankers carrying more than 300,000 tons that could be bound for the United States, with arrival dates running from November 7 to November 18, according to RFE/RL reporting carried on November 3. Even a clean sweep of those arrivals changes the month, not the market.

My position, and what settles it

My position: until a discharge is documented, treat this deal as a headline with tankers attached. The investable read runs opposite to the October consensus. Every week the cargoes circle, East Coast diesel stays short, and the crack spread that refiners such as Phillips 66 earn stays wide. Phillips 66 sits on both sides of the letter Sherrill sent: a named terminal operator warned off the cargo, and a refiner paid by the shortage the cargo was meant to cure. Watch three markers. First, a confirmed US discharge with a named terminal and buyer, the only event that turns the licence into supply. Second, the eight Ukraine monitored tankers as their November 7 to 18 arrival window closes. Third, the House bill to nullify General License 135, which would end the argument by statute. If the first marker lands, I will revisit the crack. Until then, the ships are the data, and the ships are not landing.
