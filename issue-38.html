Issue #38 Free Article Energy Leverage

The Surplus Is Crude. The Shortage Is Diesel.

Marcus Vail Resilience Alpha Research November 15, 2026 4 min read

The International Energy Agency published its November Oil Market Report on November 13, 2026, and the headline travels fast: global oil supply passed demand in October for the first time since the Iran war began. Supply reached 105.4 million barrels a day, up 2.3 million barrels a day on September and up 5.3 million since August. Observed inventories rose by 52 million barrels in October, or 1.7 million barrels a day. The report is here.

The consensus read

The consensus read is that the war premium is finished. North Sea Dated crude has fallen by more than 30 percent, from a high of $136 a barrel on October 8 to around $93 at the time the IEA wrote. Brent futures settled at $95.84 a barrel on Friday, down about 2.5 percent for the week, according to Reuters reporting. The IEA raised its fourth quarter supply estimate by 3.3 million barrels a day in a single report, pointed to a surplus in the fourth quarter and a larger build in 2027, and projected supply of 108.1 million barrels a day next year. On those numbers, crude length looks crowded and late.

Where the consensus stops reading

Keep reading the same report and a second market appears. Gulf exports excluding Oman reached 18.4 million barrels a day in October, still 26 percent below pre-war levels. The crude portion recovered to 15 million barrels a day, equal to 82 percent of pre-war flows. Gulf exports of refined products and LPG sit at roughly half of pre-war levels, with no recovery date in the report. Global refinery throughput fell by 1.3 million barrels a day in October to 81.3 million, a seasonal low. European diesel held near $180 a barrel, down about 11 percent from its recent high and still 77 percent above its pre-war price. The IEA states the point in plain language: severe tightness in product supply, especially diesel, remains the overriding concern, and an accelerated release of emergency diesel stocks is the bridge until refinery output recovers.

The texture underneath

Part of the bottleneck has an address in Russia. The IEA says persistent attacks on Russian energy infrastructure continue to choke off diesel supplies to international markets. The Financial Times reported on November 10 that the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air estimates 20 percent to 35 percent of Russian refining capacity is idle, after at least eight refineries were hit following the resumption of Ukrainian strikes on October 30. Five of those plants are wholly or jointly owned by Rosneft. Rosneft has capped gasoline sales at 30 litres per vehicle in cities and 50 litres on highways, in place since November 7 in at least four regions, including Zabaykalsky Krai in the Far East. In Nizhny Novgorod, stations limited sales and banned purchases by vehicles registered in other regions. A country rationing gasoline at the pump is a poor candidate to refill the world diesel market.

The one number

87 dollars. That is the gap between North Sea Dated crude at around $93 a barrel and European diesel near $180 a barrel in the same IEA report. Crude is the input. Diesel is what trucks, farms, ships and generators burn. The $87 gap is the rent the refining system collects while crude piles up on water and product tanks stay thin. Until that gap closes, the surplus headline describes a barrel few end users can use in the form it sits.

My position, and what to watch

My position: the surplus is real, and it is a crude statement only. Trading the IEA headline as proof that the energy shock has ended repeats the error of pricing Brent on November 11 while tankers burned, in reverse. The scarcity moved downstream, from the wellhead to the refinery gate, and the money followed it. The IEA notes that Atlantic Basin refineries, particularly in the United States, are running hard to capture high margins. That sentence is the trade. Watch four markers. First, December refinery throughput against the IEA forecast of 84.7 million barrels a day, up from 81.3 million in October. Second, European diesel: a fall from near $180 toward crude would confirm healing, and persistence near $180 confirms the bottleneck. Third, Gulf product and LPG exports rising from roughly half of pre-war levels. Fourth, the pace of emergency diesel stock releases, because governments do not accelerate releases into a market that has healed. The next IEA report lands in mid-December.
